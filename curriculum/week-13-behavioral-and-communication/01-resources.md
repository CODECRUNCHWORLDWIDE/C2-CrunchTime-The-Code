# Week 13 — Resources

Every resource is **free** and **publicly accessible**.

## Required reading (work it into your week)

- **The STAR method — official UK National Careers / широко published explainer**: search "STAR method interview" on any reputable careers site (the UK government careers service, university career-center pages, and most large-company careers blogs publish near-identical breakdowns). The single most important detail: STAR is **Situation, Task, Action, Result**, and the Action is the part you are graded on — it should carry the majority of your airtime. Most candidates over-invest in Situation and under-invest in Action and Result.
- **"Tell me about yourself" structures** — search any university career center's interview guide. The reusable shape is *present → past → future*: what you do now, the path that got you here, why this role is the next step. Ninety seconds, not a résumé recital.
- **Your own résumé and project history** — re-read it line by line. Every bullet is a candidate STAR story. The mining exercise in Lecture 1 §6 starts from your own document, not from a generic list. The most authentic stories are the ones already on your page; you have just never told them in STAR.
- **The company's engineering blog and careers page** — for any company you are interviewing with. The "questions you ask the interviewer" (Lecture 3 §4) are sourced here: a question about a specific system you read about on their blog signals genuine research and is scored as engagement.
- **A timer / voice recorder** — your phone is enough. Every story in your bank gets timed and recorded at least once. A story that "feels like two minutes" is usually three. The recorder is to the behavioral round what the self-test block was to the coding exercises: the objective check.

## On the round itself

The behavioral round is a structured assessment, not a personality test. The interviewer is scoring you against named competencies and writing the scores into the same packet as your coding scores. Understanding *what they are scoring* is the recognition skill.

- **"Tell me about a time you disagreed with someone..."** — Conflict / disagreement category. They are scoring whether you can disagree professionally, hear the other side, and reach a resolution without either steamrolling or caving. The trap is telling a story where you were simply right and the other person was simply wrong; the strong answer shows you genuinely engaged with their position.
- **"Tell me about a failure / a mistake you made..."** — Failure / mistake category. They are scoring ownership and learning, *not* whether you have failed (everyone has). The trap is the fake failure ("I work too hard") or blaming others; the strong answer owns a real mistake with real stakes and states the concrete change you made afterward.
- **"Tell me about a time you led / took initiative..."** — Leadership / initiative category. They are scoring whether you move things forward without being told to. You do not need a manager title; leading a design discussion, unblocking a stuck teammate, or driving a decision all count.
- **"Tell me about a time you worked on a team / handled a difficult teammate..."** — Teamwork / collaboration category. They are scoring whether you make the people around you more effective. The trap is a story that is secretly all about you; the strong answer credits the team while making your specific contribution clear.
- **"Tell me about a time the requirements were unclear / you had to figure out what to build..."** — Ambiguity category. They are scoring whether you can make progress without a complete spec. The strong answer shows you structured the ambiguity (asked the right questions, made and validated assumptions, shipped something to learn from) rather than freezing.
- **"Tell me about a time you were under a tight deadline / had to cut scope..."** — Pressure / deadlines category. They are scoring judgment under constraint: what you cut, what you protected, how you communicated the trade-off.
- **"Tell me about a time you convinced someone / changed a decision without authority..."** — Influence category. They are scoring whether you can move a decision through persuasion and data rather than position.
- **"Tell me about yourself / a time you received hard feedback / how you've grown..."** — Growth / self-awareness category, plus the opener. They are scoring self-awareness and the capacity to change in response to feedback.

If the prompt names a *negative* (conflict, failure, mistake, pressure), the competency being scored is your *composure and learning*, not whether the negative happened. If the prompt names a *positive* (leadership, influence, initiative), the competency is your *agency*. Mapping the prompt to the competency is the Match step.

## Free preparation platforms and references

- **LeetCode — behavioral / "Tell me about" discussion threads** (free): the discuss section has crowd-sourced lists of the most-asked behavioral prompts at major companies. Use them as a question bank to test your story-bank coverage, not as scripts to memorize.
- **Glassdoor — interview question reports** (free with account): filter to a target company and role; the behavioral questions reported there are real and current. Cross-check your coverage matrix against the ten most-reported prompts for your target.
- **Pramp / interviewing.io free mock rounds** (free tiers): both offer peer or volunteer behavioral mocks. A live mock is worth more than any written drill; book one for Friday or the weekend.
- **Your university career center** (free for students and often alumni): mock-interview services, résumé reviews, and behavioral-prep workshops. Underused and high-value.
- **The "Amazon Leadership Principles" public page** — even if you are not interviewing at Amazon, the sixteen principles are the most explicit published list of behavioral competencies anywhere, and mapping your stories to them is the single best coverage exercise available for free.

## The eight categories — quick reference

The Match step for behavioral. Memorize the categories and their signal phrases; naming the category in five seconds is the whole game.

| # | Category | Signal phrases | What they score |
|---|----------|----------------|-----------------|
| 1 | Conflict / disagreement | "disagreed with," "pushed back," "didn't see eye to eye" | Professional disagreement; engaging the other side; resolution |
| 2 | Failure / mistake | "a mistake," "a failure," "something that went wrong," "would do differently" | Ownership; concrete learning; composure |
| 3 | Leadership / initiative | "took the lead," "drove," "initiative," "stepped up," "no one was assigned" | Agency; moving things forward unprompted |
| 4 | Teamwork / collaboration | "on a team," "a difficult teammate," "collaborated," "helped a colleague" | Making others effective; crediting the team |
| 5 | Ambiguity / undefined problems | "requirements were unclear," "figure out what to build," "no clear path" | Structuring ambiguity; progress without a spec |
| 6 | Pressure / deadlines / scope | "tight deadline," "under pressure," "had to cut scope," "competing priorities" | Judgment under constraint; trade-off communication |
| 7 | Influence / persuasion | "convinced," "changed their mind," "without authority," "got buy-in" | Persuasion via data and trust, not position |
| 8 | Growth / feedback / "about yourself" | "tell me about yourself," "hard feedback," "how you've grown," "a weakness" | Self-awareness; change in response to feedback |

If a prompt seems to fit two categories (e.g. a conflict that was resolved by influence), pick the *dominant* competency — the one the verb in the prompt points at — and frame the story to that, while letting the secondary competency show through. One story, deliberately framed, can answer either.

## STAR — the structure cheatsheet

The shape you walk in your head on every behavioral answer.

| Letter | What you say | Airtime | The discipline |
|--------|--------------|--------:|----------------|
| **S — Situation** | One or two sentences of context: the project, the team, the stakes. | ~15% | Enough to make the rest legible; no more. The trap is a two-minute setup. |
| **T — Task** | Your specific responsibility — what *you* were on the hook for. | ~10% | "I" not "we." This is the line most candidates skip; it sets up the Action. |
| **A — Action** | The decisions and steps *you personally* took, in order. | ~60% | First-person, concrete, sequential. This is what they score. No "we shipped"; "I proposed / I built / I paired with." |
| **R — Result** | The outcome, quantified where possible, plus the lesson. | ~15% | Always land it. A buried or missing Result negates a strong Action. End on the metric or the concrete observable. |

Three observations:

1. **The Action is the answer.** Sixty percent of your airtime goes to what *you did*. A common failure is a beautiful Situation, a clear Task, and then a one-sentence Action followed by a vague Result. Invert that instinct: rush nothing in the Action, and never skip the Result.
2. **"I" beats "we" every time it is honest.** The interviewer is scoring *your* contribution. Crediting the team is good manners and good signal; replacing your contribution with the team's is a scoring error. Say "the team shipped X; my piece was Y" — both, in that order.
3. **The Result is non-negotiable.** Even a failure story has a Result: what changed because of the lesson. A story with no Result is an anecdote, not an answer. If you cannot state the Result, the story is not bank-ready.

### The canonical STAR skeleton

```
SITUATION  (1–2 sentences)
  "On [project], we were [context], and [the stakes / the problem]."

TASK       (1 sentence)
  "I was responsible for [your specific piece]."

ACTION     (the bulk — 3 to 5 concrete steps, first-person)
  "I started by [step 1]. When [obstacle], I [decision].
   I [step 2], pairing with [person] to [why].
   I [step 3], which meant [trade-off you made deliberately]."

RESULT     (1–2 sentences, quantified)
  "As a result, [outcome with a number or a concrete observable].
   Afterward I [the lasting change / what you carried forward]."
```

Memorize the cadence, not a script. The skeleton is the same for every category; only the content changes. Under pressure, the skeleton is what keeps you from rambling.

## On the story bank

A short table of the twelve story *slots* the mini-project asks you to fill. The handles are yours; these are the coverage targets.

| Slot | Story archetype | Primary category | Also covers |
|------|-----------------|------------------|-------------|
| 1 | A real disagreement you resolved | Conflict | Influence, Teamwork |
| 2 | A mistake with real stakes | Failure | Growth, Pressure |
| 3 | Something you drove unprompted | Leadership | Initiative, Ambiguity |
| 4 | A difficult teammate you worked with | Teamwork | Conflict, Influence |
| 5 | An underspecified problem you shipped | Ambiguity | Leadership, Initiative |
| 6 | A deadline crunch where you cut scope | Pressure | Leadership, Failure |
| 7 | A decision you changed via data | Influence | Conflict, Leadership |
| 8 | Hard feedback you acted on | Growth | Failure, Teamwork |
| 9 | A win you are proud of | Leadership | Initiative, Influence |
| 10 | A time you helped someone else succeed | Teamwork | Leadership, Growth |
| 11 | A time you learned a hard technical lesson | Failure | Growth, Ambiguity |
| 12 | The "tell me about yourself" arc | Growth (opener) | — |

Two observations:

1. **Twelve stories, ~thirty prompts.** Because each story covers two to three categories, twelve well-chosen stories answer far more than twelve questions. The mini-project's coverage matrix is the proof that your twelve cover all eight categories with depth to spare.
2. **Slot 12 is the opener, not a category.** "Tell me about yourself" is not one of the eight scored categories; it is the frame that opens the round. It still earns a bank slot because it is asked in nearly every loop and rewards rehearsal as much as any STAR story.

## Glossary additions

- **Behavioral round** — the non-coding interview that scores soft competencies (conflict, ownership, leadership, ambiguity, influence) against a rubric. Scored in the same hiring packet as the coding rounds.
- **STAR** — Situation, Task, Action, Result. The four-part structure for a behavioral answer. The Action carries the majority of the airtime; the Result must always land.
- **Story bank** — a personal, pre-written, rehearsed set of STAR anecdotes drawn from real experience, each cross-referenced to the question categories it covers. The week's central artifact.
- **Handle** — a short nickname for a bank story (e.g. "the migration rollback") used to retrieve it quickly under pressure. You think the handle, then deliver the story.
- **Coverage matrix** — a grid of stories (rows) against the eight categories (columns); a checked cell means the story can answer that category. The acceptance bar is ≥ 2 stories per category and ≥ 2 categories per story.
- **The eight categories** — Conflict, Failure, Leadership, Teamwork, Ambiguity, Pressure, Influence, Growth. The Match-step taxonomy for behavioral prompts.
- **Task/Action split** — the STAR discipline of separating what you were *responsible for* (Task) from what you *personally did* (Action). The most-missed STAR distinction.
- **"I vs. we"** — the discipline of describing your own contribution in the first person singular. Credit the team (manners + signal), but never let "we" erase what *you* did (the thing being scored).
- **Recovery** — the technique of abandoning or re-framing a story mid-answer when you realize it does not fit the prompt, without panic or repeated apology.
- **Follow-up email** — the brief, specific message sent within 24 hours of an interview, referencing one concrete moment and reinforcing one reason you fit. Not a generic thank-you template.
- **The opener** — "tell me about yourself." The 90-second present-past-future narrative that frames the loop. Rehearsed, not improvised.
- **Signal phrase** — the verb or phrase in a prompt that identifies its category ("disagreed with" → Conflict; "took the lead" → Leadership). The fastest route to the five-second Match.

## Cheatsheet — the behavioral recognition flowchart

A short decision flowchart you should be able to walk in five seconds when you hear a prompt.

```
What does the verb in the prompt point at?

A negative event (conflict, mistake, failure, pressure)?
  "disagreed / pushed back"        -> Conflict.    Story: a resolved disagreement.
  "mistake / failure / went wrong" -> Failure.     Story: real stakes, real lesson.
  "tight deadline / cut scope"     -> Pressure.    Story: a trade-off you made and communicated.

A positive event (agency, persuasion)?
  "led / drove / took initiative"  -> Leadership.  Story: something you moved unprompted.
  "convinced / got buy-in"         -> Influence.   Story: a decision you changed with data.

A collaboration event?
  "on a team / difficult teammate" -> Teamwork.    Story: you made others effective.

An open-ended event?
  "requirements unclear / figure out" -> Ambiguity. Story: progress without a spec.

A self-reflective prompt?
  "tell me about yourself"         -> Opener.      present -> past -> future, 90s.
  "hard feedback / how you've grown / a weakness" -> Growth. Story: feedback you acted on.

Then, having named the category:
  Pull the bank story tagged to that category.
  Deliver in STAR: Situation (short) -> Task (your piece) -> Action (60%) -> Result (land it).
  If two sentences in it does not fit -> pivot (see Lecture 3 §1).
```

Read aloud; should hit four to five seconds for the classification. The order matters — the verb in the prompt is the fastest discriminator, exactly as the constraint signal is in a coding prompt.
