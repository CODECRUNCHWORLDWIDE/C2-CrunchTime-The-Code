# Lecture 1 — The Eight Categories and STAR

> **Duration:** ~2 hours.
> **Outcome:** You can classify any behavioral prompt into one of eight categories within five seconds, structure any answer in STAR with the Action carrying the majority of the airtime and the Result always landed, separate the Task from the Action and "I" from "we," and mine your own real experience for legitimate STAR material even if your history feels thin.

For twelve weeks this course has trained one reflex: hear a problem, name the pattern, run the method. UMPIRE is that method for code. This week installs the same reflex for the conversation that decides your offer just as firmly as the whiteboard does — and it installs it with the same machinery: a Match step (the eight categories), an Implement step (STAR), and a preparation artifact you build in advance (the story bank).

The mistake almost every candidate makes is treating the behavioral round as unpreparable — a vibe check, a personality test, luck. It is none of those. It is a **structured assessment**. The interviewer has a rubric with named competencies on it, and they are scoring your answers and writing those scores into the same packet that holds your coding scores. A strong-no-hire on behavioral sinks a clean coding loop at most companies. You would never walk into the coding round without a method; do not walk into the behavioral round without one either.

By the end of this lecture you should be able to hear any prompt and, within five seconds, say to yourself: "**this is a [category] question**," and then, "**the story I'm pulling is [handle], and I'll deliver it in STAR.**" The classification is the work of the first hour. STAR structure and the Task/Action discipline are the work of the second.

This lecture covers the foundations: the eight categories, the STAR format, the Task/Action split, the "I vs. we" discipline, and how to mine thin experience. Lecture 2 covers the story bank and the coverage matrix. Lecture 3 covers recovery, the opener, and the follow-up email.

## 1. Why the behavioral round is real, and gradeable

Three facts most candidates do not internalize until they have lost an offer to them.

**Fact one: it is scored against named competencies.** The interviewer is not forming a vague impression. They have a rubric — often a literal form — with rows like *ownership*, *conflict resolution*, *dealing with ambiguity*, *bias for action*, *ability to influence*. After your answer, they assign a score and write a justification. Those scores are aggregated across the loop. The behavioral round is as quantified as the coding round; it just hides the rubric from you.

**Fact two: it filters.** At most large companies, the behavioral score can independently sink the loop. A candidate who solves both coding problems cleanly but reads as someone who blames teammates, cannot take feedback, or freezes under ambiguity will get a no-hire. The behavioral round is not a tiebreaker you can coast through; it is a gate.

**Fact three: it is preparable — exactly like the coding round.** The objection "but I can't predict the questions" is false. There are eight categories. There are perhaps thirty common phrasings. A bank of twelve stories covers all of them. The interviewer asking "tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate" is asking the *same category* as "describe a conflict you navigated" and "tell me about a time you pushed back on a decision." One prepared story answers all three. The variance is in the surface phrasing; the underlying categories are fixed.

That third fact is the whole week. You are not memorizing answers to specific questions. You are building a small, well-organized inventory of real stories and learning to retrieve and deliver the right one on demand.

## 2. The eight categories

Every behavioral prompt is one of eight question types. Naming the type is your Match step.

**1. Conflict / disagreement.** *"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a coworker / your manager / a technical decision."* The competency: can you disagree professionally — hear the other side, engage their actual argument, and reach a resolution — without steamrolling or caving? The trap is the story where you were simply right and they were simply wrong; that shows you do not genuinely engage opposing views. The strong story shows real engagement with their position and a resolution that was better than either starting point.

**2. Failure / mistake.** *"Tell me about a failure. A mistake you made. Something you'd do differently."* The competency: ownership and learning. Note what is *not* being scored: whether you have failed. Everyone has; claiming otherwise reads as either dishonest or inexperienced. The traps are the fake failure ("I'm a perfectionist," "I work too hard") and blame-shifting ("the failure was really my manager's fault"). The strong story owns a real mistake with real stakes and states the concrete, specific change you made because of it.

**3. Leadership / initiative.** *"Tell me about a time you took the lead / drove a project / stepped up when no one else would."* The competency: agency — do you move things forward without being told to? You do **not** need a manager title. Leading a design discussion, unblocking a stuck teammate, noticing a gap and filling it, driving a decision to closure — all are leadership. The trap is waiting to have held a formal leadership role; the strong story is any moment you created motion that would not have happened without you.

**4. Teamwork / collaboration.** *"Tell me about a time you worked on a team / dealt with a difficult teammate / helped a colleague."* The competency: do you make the people around you more effective? The trap is a "teamwork" story that is secretly all about you, where the team is set dressing. The strong story credits the team's contribution clearly *and* makes your specific role unmistakable — both, not one.

**5. Ambiguity / undefined problems.** *"Tell me about a time the requirements were unclear / you had to figure out what to build / there was no clear path."* The competency: can you make progress without a complete spec? The trap is a story where the ambiguity resolved itself or someone else resolved it. The strong story shows you *structured* the ambiguity — asked the sharpest questions, made and validated assumptions, shipped something small to learn from — rather than freezing or waiting.

**6. Pressure / deadlines / scope.** *"Tell me about a tight deadline / a time you were under pressure / a time you had to cut scope."* The competency: judgment under constraint. What did you cut, what did you protect, and how did you communicate the trade-off? The trap is the hero story where you worked 80 hours and saved the day through sheer effort; that signals poor planning, not good judgment. The strong story shows a deliberate trade-off, communicated early.

**7. Influence / persuasion without authority.** *"Tell me about a time you convinced someone / changed a decision / got buy-in without being in charge."* The competency: can you move a decision through persuasion, data, and trust rather than position? The trap is "I told them to and they did it" (that is authority, not influence). The strong story shows you changed someone's mind by understanding their concern and bringing evidence.

**8. Growth / feedback / "tell me about yourself."** *"Tell me about yourself. A time you got hard feedback. A weakness. How you've grown."* The competency: self-awareness and the capacity to change. The opener ("tell me about yourself") is its own special case — covered in Lecture 3. The feedback variant's trap is the humble-brag weakness; the strong story names a real area, the feedback that surfaced it, and what you did differently afterward.

**The five-second classification.** Listen for the verb. *Disagreed, pushed back* → Conflict. *Mistake, failure, went wrong* → Failure. *Led, drove, took initiative* → Leadership. *Team, teammate, collaborated* → Teamwork. *Unclear, figure out, no spec* → Ambiguity. *Deadline, pressure, cut scope* → Pressure. *Convinced, buy-in, changed their mind* → Influence. *Tell me about yourself, feedback, grown, weakness* → Growth. The verb is the fastest discriminator, the same way the constraint signal is in a coding prompt.

**When a prompt straddles two categories.** Some prompts genuinely fit two — "tell me about a conflict you resolved by bringing data" is both Conflict and Influence. Pick the *dominant* competency — the one the verb points at — and frame your story to it, letting the secondary competency show through. This is a feature, not a problem: it is exactly why one story can cover multiple categories, which is the foundation of the story bank.

## 3. STAR — the structure

STAR is the Implement step. Once you have named the category and pulled a story, STAR is the structure that delivers it without rambling. Four parts.

**S — Situation (≈15% of airtime, one to two sentences).** Just enough context to make the rest legible: the project, the team, the stakes. The single most common STAR failure is a Situation that runs two minutes — the candidate sets up the world in exhaustive detail and never gets to what they *did*. Resist it. The Situation is scaffolding; build it fast and move on.

> *"Last summer I interned on the payments team. Two weeks before launch we found that our reconciliation job silently dropped about 2% of transactions under load."*

That is a complete Situation. Project, stakes, problem — in two sentences.

**T — Task (≈10%, one sentence).** Your *specific* responsibility. Not the team's mission — yours. This is the line most candidates skip entirely, and skipping it is a mistake, because the Task is what makes the Action legible as *your* contribution rather than the team's.

> *"I owned the reconciliation job, so finding and fixing the data loss before launch was on me."*

Notice the "I" and the "on me." The Task sets up everything that follows as your work.

**A — Action (≈60%, the bulk).** What *you personally* did, in order, with concrete decisions. First person singular. This is the part that gets scored, so it gets the most airtime. Sequence it: "I started by... when I hit X, I decided Y... I then... which meant trading off Z deliberately." Each step should be a real decision a listener can evaluate.

> *"I started by reproducing it with a load test, which confirmed the drops happened only above ~500 transactions per second. I traced it to an un-batched database write that timed out silently. I had two options: add retry logic, or switch to batched writes. Retries would have shipped faster but masked the root cause, so I chose batching even though it meant rewriting the writer. I paired with the senior engineer who owned the schema to make sure the batch size respected the connection-pool limits, and I added an alert so a silent drop could never happen unnoticed again."*

That is the Action: concrete, sequential, first-person, full of real decisions and one deliberate trade-off. This is what earns the score.

**R — Result (≈15%, one to two sentences).** The outcome, quantified where possible, plus what you carried forward. **Always land it.** An Action with no Result is a story that trails off, and a trailing-off story negates a strong Action — the interviewer is left without the payoff they score on.

> *"We launched on the original date with zero dropped transactions in the first month, and the alert I added caught an unrelated regression three weeks later. I took away a rule I still use: never ship a fix that hides the symptom instead of removing the cause."*

Outcome (quantified: zero drops, caught a later regression, shipped on date) plus the lasting lesson. The Result is non-negotiable; rehearse it until you cannot forget to deliver it.

**The airtime budget is the discipline.** Roughly 15 / 10 / 60 / 15. The instinct most people have is the inverse — long Situation, short Action — and inverting that instinct is half of what this week teaches. Say the Situation fast, name the Task, spend your time in the Action, and always close on the Result.

## 4. The Task/Action split, and "I vs. we"

Two related disciplines that separate strong answers from average ones.

**The Task/Action split.** The Task is what you were *responsible for*; the Action is what you *did*. They feel similar but they are different, and keeping them distinct is the most-missed STAR detail. The Task is one sentence that establishes accountability — "I owned X." The Action is the body — "here is what I did about X." Skipping the Task makes the Action float free of ownership; the interviewer cannot tell whether the impressive work was yours or the team's.

**"I vs. we."** The interviewer scores *your* contribution. This produces a rule that feels rude at first and is in fact correct: in the Action, say "I," not "we." When you say "we shipped the feature," you have told the interviewer nothing about what *you* did — a passenger and the driver both say "we shipped it." Say instead: "the team shipped the feature; my piece was the rollback plan and the load test." That sentence credits the team (manners and good signal) and names your contribution (the thing being scored) — both, in that order.

The failure mode is the candidate who, out of modesty or habit, narrates a great project entirely in "we" and walks out having earned nothing, because the interviewer genuinely cannot extract what *they* contributed. Modesty is a virtue in life and a scoring error in the behavioral round. Be generous to your team and precise about yourself.

A quick self-check while rehearsing: in your Action, count the "I"s and the "we"s. If "we" dominates, rewrite. Every "we did X" that was actually "I did X" is a point you are leaving on the table.

## 5. Quantifying the Result (preview)

The Result lands hardest when it carries a number. "Reduced on-call pages from about twelve a week to two." "Cut the build from nine minutes to ninety seconds." "Shipped on the original date with zero data loss." Numbers are concrete, defensible, and memorable; "it went well" is none of those.

But many real contributions are not naturally numeric, and inventing a fake metric is worse than none — it collapses under a follow-up. When there is no honest number, substitute a **concrete observable**: "the design doc I wrote became the team's template for the next three projects," "the runbook I created meant the next on-call didn't have to escalate," "the bug never recurred." A concrete observable outcome is a legitimate, strong Result. The bar is *specificity*, not numbers per se. Lecture 2 §3 drills this; for now, know that every Result must be either quantified or concretely observable, and "it was a success" is neither.

## 6. Mining thin experience for STAR material

The most common objection in this week: "I don't have stories — I'm a student / career-changer / I've only had one internship." This is almost always false, and the belief that it is true is itself the problem. You do not lack stories; you lack the habit of seeing your experiences *as* stories. Here is how to mine.

**Start from your résumé.** Every bullet on your résumé is a candidate STAR story. "Built a REST API for the inventory service" → what was hard about it? what decision did you make? what was unclear? what went wrong and how did you fix it? Each bullet, interrogated, yields one or more of the eight categories. Go line by line; you will find more material than you expected.

**Course projects count.** A semester-long capstone, a group project where a teammate went dark, a database course where you had to make a schema decision with incomplete requirements — these are legitimate Conflict, Teamwork, and Ambiguity stories. The interviewer does not require the story to come from paid industry work; they require it to be *real* and to show the competency. A well-told capstone conflict beats a vague industry conflict.

**Open source, hackathons, clubs, research, side projects.** A hackathon where you cut scope at 2 a.m. is a Pressure story. A PR review where you pushed back on a maintainer's suggestion is a Conflict story. Leading a student club's tooling migration is a Leadership story. Research where the problem was undefined for the first month is an Ambiguity story. All count.

**The interrogation questions.** For any experience, ask: *What was the hardest decision I made here? What did I disagree with someone about? What went wrong, and what did I do? What was unclear at the start? What did I do that wouldn't have happened without me? What did I learn that changed how I work?* Each question maps to a category, and each yields raw material for a STAR story.

**The honesty rule.** Every story is true. Fabrication collapses under follow-up — interviewers ask "what did they say back?", "what would you do differently?", "how did the teammate react?" precisely to test authenticity, and invented stories run out of detail fast. Embellishment is a losing strategy. The good news is you do not need to embellish: a true story, well-structured in STAR, with a landed result, is more than enough. The craft this week is in the *telling*, not the inventing.

## 7. Worked example — one prompt, full STAR

Prompt: *"Tell me about a time you disagreed with a technical decision."* (Category: Conflict.)

> **Situation.** *"On my internship's notification service, the team decided to send all emails synchronously inside the request handler. I thought that would make the API slow and fragile under load."*
>
> **Task.** *"I was the one implementing the email-send path, so I had to either build it their way or make the case for a different one."*
>
> **Action.** *"Rather than just objecting, I asked the tech lead what was driving the synchronous choice — it turned out to be a worry that a queue would add operational complexity the small team couldn't support. That was a fair concern, so I didn't dismiss it. I wrote a short load test showing the synchronous path added 400ms per request and failed entirely when the email provider was slow. Then I proposed a middle path: a lightweight in-process background task with a retry, no new infrastructure, which addressed the complexity worry while fixing the latency. I walked the lead through the test results and the proposal in a fifteen-minute pairing session."*
>
> **Result.** *"They agreed, we shipped the background-task version, and p99 request latency dropped from about 600ms to under 100ms. The bigger thing I took away: the fastest way to win a technical disagreement is to understand the other person's actual constraint, not to argue harder for my position."*

Note the structure. Short Situation. One-sentence Task establishing ownership. A long Action that engages the *other side's* concern (the senior move for a Conflict story), brings data, and proposes a resolution. A Result with a number and a lesson. Under two minutes spoken. This is the target for every story in your bank.

## 8. What to take into Lecture 2

- The behavioral round is scored against a rubric and can independently sink a loop. It is preparable exactly like the coding round.
- Eight categories. Classify any prompt in five seconds by listening for the verb.
- STAR: Situation (15%), Task (10%), Action (60%), Result (15%). Action is what gets scored; Result must always land.
- Separate Task (what you owned) from Action (what you did). Say "I" in the Action; credit the team without erasing yourself.
- Quantify the Result, or substitute a concrete observable. "It went well" is not a Result.
- You have more stories than you think. Mine your résumé, projects, and coursework with the interrogation questions. Every story is true.

Lecture 2 turns these foundations into the week's central artifact: a story bank of twelve anecdotes, each cross-referenced to the categories it covers, proven complete by a coverage matrix. The Match-and-Implement reflex you built here is what the bank lets you run on demand.
