# Exercise 2 — Failure / Mistake (STAR)

> **Category:** Failure / mistake. **Target time:** 30 min to draft and revise; the spoken answer must come in under 2:00.

## STAR checklist (read before drafting)

- [ ] **Situation** is one to two sentences and makes the stakes clear (a real failure has real stakes).
- [ ] **Task** names what you were responsible for — the thing the failure was *yours* to own.
- [ ] **Action** owns the mistake plainly, then describes what *you* did to address it. No blame-shifting.
- [ ] **Result** states the immediate outcome *and* the concrete, specific change you made afterward so it would not recur.
- [ ] The failure is **real** and has **real stakes**. Not a humble-brag ("I work too hard").
- [ ] Spoken aloud, it comes in under **2:00**. You timed it.

---

## The prompt

> **"Tell me about a significant mistake you made or a time you failed. What happened, and what did you do about it?"**

The canonical Failure prompt. Variants: "a time something went wrong," "something you'd do differently," "a project that didn't go as planned." All one category.

## What this question scores

The competency is **ownership and learning**. The interviewer already knows you have failed — everyone has, and a candidate who claims otherwise reads as inexperienced or dishonest. They are scoring whether you *own* your failures and whether you *learn* from them. The whole question is a test of those two things; the failure itself is just the vehicle.

## The traps to avoid

Two classic ones.

- **The fake failure.** "My biggest weakness is that I'm a perfectionist" or "I care too much about quality." These read as evasions and score near zero. The question wants a *real* mistake with *real* stakes.
- **The blame-shift.** "The failure was really because my manager gave bad requirements / my teammate dropped the ball." Even when partly true, blaming others answers the question backwards — it scores *anti*-ownership. Own your piece, however small the share.

The strong version: a genuine mistake you made, stated without flinching, followed by exactly what you changed because of it. Composure while telling it is itself part of the signal — a candidate who can discuss a real failure calmly reads as senior.
