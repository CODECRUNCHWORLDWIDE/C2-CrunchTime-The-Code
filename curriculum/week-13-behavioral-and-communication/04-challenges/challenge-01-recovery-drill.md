# Challenge 1 — The Recovery Drill

> **Difficulty:** Hard (it requires deliberately putting yourself in a bad spot and practicing your way out). **Target time:** 60 minutes including the reflection write-up.

This is the deep-dive on the single most under-practiced behavioral skill: **recovering when you realize, mid-answer, that you pulled the wrong story.** Everyone hits this in a real loop — you hear "conflict," start a story, and two sentences in realize it is really about ambiguity. The candidates who recover cleanly look *more* impressive than candidates who never stumbled, because composure under a small real-time adversity is exactly what the round is built to surface. The candidates who flail — freeze, apologize repeatedly, trail off — can lose the question outright.

You cannot build this skill by reading about it. You build it by reps, the same way you built UMPIRE. This challenge is the rep.

---

## The three recovery moves (from Lecture 3 §1)

1. **Re-frame in place.** Bend the same story to the asked competency without restarting. The cleanest move; works when the story *can* be re-framed. Most recoveries should be this.
2. **Name the switch and pivot.** "Actually, let me give you a cleaner example of that." Then start a different story. The *naming* makes it read as deliberate, not panicked.
3. **Finish briefly and bridge.** When you are too deep to abandon: finish the story fast and explicitly connect its tail to the asked competency.

---

## The drill

You will run **three rounds**, one per recovery move. Each round, you *deliberately* start a story that does not fit the prompt, then practice the move. The point is to make the recovery feel routine so that when it happens for real — involuntarily — you have muscle memory instead of panic.

### Setup

- **With a peer (preferred):** give your peer the three prompts below and a copy of your story-bank handles. They ask a prompt; you start a deliberately-wrong story; you execute the assigned recovery move. Your peer notes whether the recovery read as deliberate or panicked.
- **Solo:** use a voice recorder. Read the prompt aloud, start the wrong story aloud, execute the move aloud. Then listen back and judge your own composure.

### The three rounds

**Round 1 — Re-frame in place.**
Prompt: *"Tell me about a time you showed leadership."*
Deliberately start a story you filed under **Teamwork** (helping a peer). Two sentences in, re-frame it so *you* become the engine — emphasize the part where you drove the decision or stepped into the gap — turning the teamwork story into a leadership story without restarting.

**Round 2 — Name the switch and pivot.**
Prompt: *"Tell me about a time you handled ambiguity."*
Deliberately start a story you filed under **Conflict**. Two sentences in, recognize it does not fit, and say out loud: *"Actually, a cleaner example of dealing with ambiguity is..."* Then deliver a genuine ambiguity story from your bank. The whole skill here is making the switch sound intentional.

**Round 3 — Finish briefly and bridge.**
Prompt: *"Tell me about a time you influenced a decision."*
Deliberately start a **Failure** story and get *deep* into it — four or five sentences — before you "realize" the mismatch. Now you are too far to abandon cleanly. Finish the failure story in one quick sentence, then bridge: *"...and the part that's really about influence is that I then had to convince the team to adopt the new process, which I did by..."* Convert the tail into the answer.

---

## Deliverable

A short written reflection (~300–400 words) covering:

1. **Which move felt most natural, and which felt hardest?** Most people find Re-frame easiest and the named Switch hardest (it feels exposing to say "let me give you a better example" out loud). Name your own ranking and why.
2. **Where did you still panic?** Be specific. Did your voice speed up? Did you over-apologize? Did you freeze before the pivot? The panic tells you what to drill more.
3. **What is your default recovery going to be?** Pick the one move you will reach for first in a real loop, and say why. Most people choose Re-frame as the default and keep the Switch in reserve for stories that truly cannot be bent.
4. **One concrete fix for next time.** "I'll pause half a second before recovering instead of rushing," or "I'll cut the apology entirely," or "I'll keep a mental list of which stories re-frame to which categories."

If you did the drill with a peer, include one line of their feedback per round: did the recovery read as deliberate or as a stumble?

---

## Rubric

| Dimension | Weight | What "yes" looks like |
|-----------|-------:|----------------------|
| All three moves attempted | 30% | Each round run aloud, with the assigned move, not just described on paper |
| Composure | 25% | Recoveries read as deliberate; no repeated apology, no freezing, no trailing off |
| Honest self-assessment | 25% | The reflection names a *real* panic point, not a generic "it went fine" |
| Concrete fix | 20% | A specific, actionable adjustment for the next attempt — not "practice more" |

---

## Why this is the canonical recovery skill

Three reasons.

1. **It will happen to you.** Over a full loop of four or five behavioral questions, the odds that you pull a sub-optimal story at least once are high. Recovery is not an edge case; it is a near-certainty you should be rehearsed for.
2. **It is scored as composure, which is itself a competency.** The interviewer is partly assessing how you handle small adversity in real time. A clean recovery is direct positive evidence on that axis — sometimes stronger than a clean first answer, because it is rarer and harder to fake.
3. **The named switch is the move nobody practices.** Re-framing feels safe; *announcing* a switch ("let me give you a better example") feels exposing, so candidates avoid rehearsing it and then cannot reach for it live. Drilling it here, deliberately, is what makes it available when you need it.

If you only do one challenge this week thoroughly, do this one. The follow-up email (Challenge 2) you can write at a desk any time; recovery is a live skill that only the reps build.
