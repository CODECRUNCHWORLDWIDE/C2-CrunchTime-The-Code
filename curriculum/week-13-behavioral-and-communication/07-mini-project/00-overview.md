# Mini-Project — The Story Bank (12 STAR Anecdotes + Coverage Matrix)

> The week's deliverable: a complete, rehearsed **story bank** of twelve STAR anecdotes drawn from your real experience, each given a handle and cross-referenced to the question categories it covers, plus a **coverage matrix** that proves every one of the eight behavioral categories is answerable by at least two of your stories. This is the artifact you walk into every real behavioral round carrying. It is the behavioral-week analog of the UMPIRE portfolio: the thing that turns improvisation into selection-and-delivery.

**Estimated time:** 10 hours, split across Thursday–Saturday.

This mini-project is *preparation-heavy* rather than *performance-heavy*. You will write twelve STAR stories, tag each, lay them out in a coverage matrix, find the gaps, and fill them. The bank is not graded on charisma — it is graded on coverage, structure, and honesty. By Sunday you should be able to hear any of the eight categories and name, in under three seconds, which story you would pull and how you would frame it.

---

## Why this matters

Three reasons.

1. **Coverage is what keeps you from being caught flat-footed.** The single worst behavioral-round outcome is the prompt landing on a category for which you have no ready story — you stall, you improvise, you tell something weak. The coverage matrix eliminates that outcome *by construction*: before you walk in, every category has at least two stories. This mini-project is the only deliverable in C2 whose grade is primarily about *completeness of coverage* rather than depth on a single problem, because in the behavioral round, a gap anywhere is a gap everywhere.

2. **Twelve stories cover roughly thirty prompts.** Because each story is framed for two to three categories, a well-built bank answers far more questions than it contains. The matrix is the proof. Building it forces the reuse discipline — "this conflict story is also an influence story" — that makes a small bank cover a large question space.

3. **The bank converts the in-room task from recall to selection.** Without it, every answer is: remember a relevant experience, structure it, find the result, *and* deliver fluently — all at once, under pressure. With it, the hard parts are already done at your desk; the in-room task is pick-and-deliver. That is the entire reason rehearsed candidates outperform improvising ones, and the bank is where the rehearsal lives.

## The twelve slots

Build to these coverage targets. The handles are yours; the archetypes are the targets that guarantee a balanced bank.

| Slot | Story archetype | Primary category | Should also cover |
|------|-----------------|------------------|-------------------|
| 1 | A real disagreement you resolved | Conflict | Influence, Teamwork |
| 2 | A mistake with real stakes | Failure | Growth, Pressure |
| 3 | Something you drove unprompted | Leadership | Initiative, Ambiguity |
| 4 | A difficult teammate you worked with | Teamwork | Conflict, Influence |
| 5 | An underspecified problem you shipped | Ambiguity | Leadership, Initiative |
| 6 | A deadline crunch where you cut scope | Pressure | Leadership, Failure |
| 7 | A decision you changed via data | Influence | Conflict, Leadership |
| 8 | Hard feedback you acted on | Growth | Failure, Teamwork |
| 9 | A win you are proud of | Leadership | Initiative, Influence |
| 10 | A time you helped someone else succeed | Teamwork | Leadership, Growth |
| 11 | A hard technical lesson you learned | Failure | Growth, Ambiguity |
| 12 | The "tell me about yourself" arc | Opener | — |

You may swap an archetype for a real story you have that fits a category better — the slots are a coverage scaffold, not a script. The only hard requirement is that the finished matrix satisfies both acceptance rules (below).

## STAR structure for each story

Each of slots 1–11 is a full STAR story. Slot 12 is the opener (present-past-future, ~90 seconds — see Lecture 3 §2), not a STAR story.

For each STAR story, write:

- **Handle** — a two-to-four-word retrieval nickname ("the migration rollback").
- **Tags** — every category this story can answer, with the framing noted for each (e.g. "Conflict: emphasize the disagreement with the lead; Influence: emphasize how I won the decision with data").
- **Situation** — one to two sentences. Context and stakes.
- **Task** — one sentence. Your specific responsibility, in "I."
- **Action** — the bulk. First-person, sequential, concrete decisions. This is what gets scored.
- **Result** — a metric or a concrete observable, plus the lesson. Never an adjective.

Then **time the spoken version** of each story and record whether it comes in under 2:00. A story that runs long on the page runs longer aloud; the timer is the objective check.

## The coverage matrix (the signature element)

The matrix is the deliverable that turns twelve loose stories into a proven system. Twelve rows (your stories), eight columns (the categories). Check a cell if that story can answer that category — and you have rehearsed *that framing*, not merely judged the story vaguely relevant.

```markdown
| # | Handle | Conflict | Failure | Leadership | Teamwork | Ambiguity | Pressure | Influence | Growth |
|---|--------|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| 1 | ...    |   |   |   |   |   |   |   |   |
| ...                                                                          |
| 12| opener |   |   |   |   |   |   |   | ✓ |
| | **Totals** | | | | | | | | |
```

### The two acceptance rules

1. **Every category column sums to ≥ 2.** Two stories per category means a burned story still leaves a backup, and you get to pick the better fit. A category with one story is a single point of failure.
2. **Every story row (except the opener) sums to ≥ 2.** A story that answers only one category is underused — re-frame it to surface a second competency, or replace it. The opener (slot 12) is the only allowed single-purpose row.

If the matrix fails either rule, you have found a gap. **Fill it at your desk** (Lecture 2 §6): mine a new story for a thin column, or re-interrogate an existing story for a second competency to thicken a thin row. The gap-filling *is* the work; a matrix that passes both rules on the first try usually means you tagged too loosely.

---

## How the deliverable is graded

| Dimension | Weight | What "yes" looks like |
|-----------|-------:|----------------------|
| Coverage (the matrix) | 30% | Both acceptance rules satisfied: every category ≥ 2 stories, every story (but opener) ≥ 2 categories; totals row present and correct |
| STAR structure (all stories) | 20% | Every story has all four parts; Action carries the airtime; Result is a metric or concrete observable, never an adjective |
| "I" discipline | 10% | Actions are first-person; teams credited without erasing the candidate's contribution |
| Honesty / specificity | 15% | Stories are real and detailed enough to survive follow-ups; no fabricated metrics; no humble-brag "failures" |
| Framing notes | 15% | Each tag carries a one-line note on how the story is framed for that category — proving rehearsed reuse, not loose relevance |
| Timed delivery | 10% | Each story timed aloud; over-2:00 stories flagged and trimmed |

The coverage weight is the highest, and deliberately so. In the coding weeks, depth on one problem was the grade; here, *completeness across the field* is the grade, because the behavioral round's failure mode is a gap, not a shallow answer.

## Reflection — the short README at the top of the deliverable

After the bank and matrix are complete, draft a ~300-word reflection in `story-bank/README.md`. Answer three questions:

1. **Which category was hardest to cover with two strong stories?** For most engineers it is **Influence** (plenty of "I did X" stories, few "I changed someone's mind" stories) or **Conflict** (people under-remember disagreements they handled well). Name yours and say how you filled it.
2. **Which story ended up covering the most categories, and why?** The widest-coverage stories are usually rich, multi-week experiences with conflict, ambiguity, *and* a result. Identifying yours tells you which story to lead with when you have a choice.
3. **Which story's Result was hardest to quantify, and what observable did you substitute?** This surfaces whether you have internalized that "it went well" is not a Result. Name the story and the concrete observable you landed on.

## Acceptance

The mini-project is shipped when:

- All twelve slots are filled with real, true stories (eleven STAR + the opener), committed to `behavioral/c2-week-13/story-bank/story-bank.md`.
- The coverage matrix is committed and satisfies both acceptance rules, with a totals row.
- Each story has been timed aloud, with over-2:00 stories flagged and trimmed.
- The reflection README is committed.
- The push log shows daily commits Thursday–Saturday.

Total time budget: 10 hours over three days. If you exceed 12 hours, stop and request a 1:1 with the Phase-3 lead before submitting; the over-budget usually means you are writing the stories as essays instead of STAR skeletons — the bank entries should be tight, not polished prose.

## A note on pacing

The mini-project is *not* a place to perfect prose. The bank entries are working notes you deliver *from*, not essays you read aloud. Keep each STAR story to a skeleton you can expand naturally when speaking — bullet-tight, not paragraph-smooth. Spend your time on two things: (1) the coverage matrix and its gap-filling, because coverage is the grade; and (2) timing each story aloud, because an untimed story is an unverified story.

Common over-spends: writing each story as flowing prose (write skeletons; deliver naturally), agonizing over the perfect handle (any memorable nickname works — fix it later), and over-tagging the matrix to make it look complete (tag only framings you can actually deliver; a loosely-tagged matrix is a false sense of coverage and the worst possible outcome).

If you finish the twelve-story bank under 8 hours, the stretch is to build a **second coverage matrix mapped to one specific company's named competencies** (many companies publish or are known for their behavioral priorities) and find the gaps for *that* loop specifically. Phase-3 alumni who mapped their bank to a target company's competencies before the loop reported walking in noticeably calmer, because they knew exactly which story answered each principle.
