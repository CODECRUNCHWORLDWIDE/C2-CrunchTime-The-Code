# Challenge 2 — The Follow-Up Email

> **Difficulty:** Medium (the craft is in restraint — saying less, more specifically). **Target time:** 45 minutes including the rationale.

The follow-up email is the last signal you control after an interview, and most candidates waste it on a generic thank-you template that does nothing. A good one — specific, brief, genuine — reinforces fit while you are fresh in the interviewer's mind and can tip a borderline decision. This challenge produces a polished one and the judgment to write the next one from scratch.

---

## The anatomy (from Lecture 3 §5)

Three to five sentences, sent within 24 hours.

1. **A specific thank-you** — reference one concrete moment from the conversation, not "thanks for your time."
2. **One reinforced reason you fit** — the single strongest connection between you and the role, in one sentence.
3. **A light, no-pressure close** — warm, brief, no timeline demands.

The rules, in negative form: do not grovel; do not restate your résumé; do not pressure on timeline; do not send a template.

---

## The task

Write a follow-up email to a real interview you have had, or to a realistic scenario if you have none yet. Use the scenario below if you need one.

### Scenario (use if you have no real interview)

> You interviewed with **Maya Chen**, a senior engineer on the **payments platform** team at a mid-size fintech. During the behavioral round, she spent ten minutes walking you through how the team handles **on-call for the transaction-ledger service** — specifically how they balance fast incident response against not waking people up for false alarms. You and she also connected over a shared interest in **correctness-critical backend systems**; you told her about a reconciliation pipeline you owned during an internship. The role is a backend engineering position on her team.

Write the email. Then write a 2–3 sentence **rationale** explaining: which concrete moment you referenced and why, and which single "reason you fit" you chose to reinforce and why that one over the others.

---

## Exemplar (study after drafting your own)

> **Subject:** Thank you — backend engineer conversation
>
> Hi Maya,
>
> Thank you for walking me through how your team approaches on-call for the transaction ledger — the way you balance fast incident response against alert fatigue was exactly the kind of operational detail I was hoping to learn about, and it's a problem I find genuinely interesting.
>
> Our conversation reinforced why this role appeals to me: owning the reconciliation pipeline in my internship taught me that I do my best work on correctness-critical backend systems, which is precisely what your team builds. I'd be excited to bring that to the transaction-ledger work.
>
> Thanks again for your time — I'm happy to share anything else that would be useful as you and the team consider next steps.
>
> Best,
> [Name]

### Why this exemplar works

- **Sentence one is specific** — it references the *exact* on-call topic Maya raised, which proves the candidate was present and engaged. A generic "thank you for your time" would prove nothing.
- **It reinforces exactly one reason to fit** — correctness-critical backend systems, tied to a concrete internship artifact (the reconciliation pipeline). Not five reasons; one, well-chosen.
- **The close is light** — "happy to share anything else" with no timeline pressure, no "please let me know ASAP," no groveling.
- **It is short** — under a hundred words of body. The interviewer can read it in fifteen seconds and come away with a clear, positive reinforcement.

---

## Deliverable

1. **The email** — subject line plus three-to-five-sentence body, in the anatomy above.
2. **The rationale** — 2–3 sentences: the concrete moment you referenced (and why that one), and the single fit-reason you reinforced (and why over the alternatives).

Put both in your portfolio under `behavioral/c2-week-13/challenges/follow-up-email.md`. Keep it as a template you adapt — never send verbatim — for real loops.

---

## Rubric

| Dimension | Weight | What "yes" looks like |
|-----------|-------:|----------------------|
| Specific reference | 30% | Sentence one names a *concrete* moment from the conversation, not a generic thanks |
| One reinforced fit-reason | 25% | Exactly one strong connection, tied to something concrete about you; not a résumé dump |
| Restraint | 25% | No groveling, no timeline pressure, no template feel; three-to-five sentences |
| Rationale quality | 20% | The rationale shows deliberate choice — why *that* moment and *that* fit-reason over the alternatives |

## Common failures this challenge trains out

- **The template.** "Thank you for the opportunity to interview. I am very interested in the position and look forward to hearing from you." Could have been sent to any company after any interview. Scores nothing because it signals nothing.
- **The résumé re-dump.** Re-listing every qualification. The interviewer has your résumé; the email reinforces *one* point, sharply.
- **The grovel.** "I would be so grateful for any chance — I'll do whatever it takes." Reads as desperation and lowers your standing. Write from the footing of a candidate who is also evaluating *them*.
- **The pressure.** "Could you let me know the timeline? I have other offers pending." Even when true, this belongs in a conversation with the recruiter, never in the thank-you note to the interviewer.

The discipline of this challenge is **subtraction**. The first draft is almost always too long and too generic; the polish is cutting it to one specific reference, one fit-reason, and one light close. Less, more specifically, is the whole craft.
