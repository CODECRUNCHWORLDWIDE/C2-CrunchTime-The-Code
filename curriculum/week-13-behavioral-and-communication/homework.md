# Week 13 — Homework

Six tasks plus the acceptance checklist. Allow ~5–6 hours total, spread across the week. Unlike the algorithm weeks, the highest-leverage hours here are the **recorded-and-played-back** ones — protect those. The homework feeds directly into the mini-project: the stories you refine here *are* the story bank.

The tasks are sequenced so that by Sunday you have a complete, recorded, cross-referenced story bank, a 90-second pitch, a follow-up-email template, and a reflection naming your weakest category going into Mock #3.

## Task 1 — Refine five more stories to reach twelve total

You already have five fresh stories from the drills (debugging, conflict, leadership, failure, ambiguity) and four rough drafts from your Week 1 and Week 4 homework. That's nine. **Refine the four W1/W4 drafts into full STAR write-ups** (using [`star_template.md`](./exercises/star_template.md)), and **add three more** drawn from your real projects, to reach **twelve**.

Where to find the three new ones: each substantial project usually yields two or three stories — the technical accomplishment, the conflict or ambiguity along the way, the thing that went wrong. Look especially for stories that fill categories your nine so far don't cover well (check against the matrix in Task 3). A dedicated **teamwork** story and a dedicated **difficult-person** story are the two most commonly missing.

**Acceptance.** Twelve stories committed under `behavioral/story-bank/`, each in the STAR template, each with a quantified Result and a tagged category list.

## Task 2 — Record a full behavioral round

Complete [Challenge 1](./challenges/challenge-01-full-behavioral-round.md): a continuous ~45-minute mock round of 6–8 questions, recorded, with two-pass self-feedback notes. If you have a partner, run it both directions.

**Acceptance.** The recording linked from `behavioral/mock-rounds/round-01.md`, per-answer rubric scores, three content fixes, three delivery fixes, and your two weakest answers re-recorded.

---

## Task 3 — Write the coverage matrix

Build the grid of your twelve stories (rows) against the eight categories (columns), with a ● in each cell a story strongly covers and a ○ where it's usable but not your best. The format is in [Lecture 2 §6](./lecture-notes/02-the-star-method-and-the-story-bank.md).

**Read down each column.** Every column must have at least one ●. If a column is empty, you have a gap — go back to Task 1 and write or refine a story to fill it *before* you call the homework done. An empty column is the single most important thing this matrix exists to surface.

**Acceptance.** `behavioral/story-bank/coverage-matrix.md` with all twelve stories and all eight categories, no empty column.

---

## Task 4 — Draft and record the 90-second "tell me about yourself" pitch

This is the opener for almost every round, and the one most candidates fumble by reciting their résumé. Write a ninety-second narrative with three beats:

1. **Who you are now** — one sentence: role, focus, what you're good at.
2. **The through-line** — how your last two roles connect into a story (not a chronology).
3. **Why this conversation** — what you're looking for next, tied to this kind of role.

Then **record it and cut it to under 90 seconds.** Most first takes run three minutes. Tighten until it lands under ninety and sounds like a person talking, not a bio being read.

**Acceptance.** `behavioral/pitch.md` (text) plus a recording, delivered from memory, under 90 seconds.

---

## Task 5 — Write the follow-up-email template

Write a reusable follow-up-email template you can adapt the same day after any interview. Five parts (Lecture 3 §6): specific thanks, one concrete reference to the conversation, a one-sentence fit reaffirmation, an optional one-sentence repair of anything you fumbled, under 150 words. Write it with placeholders so you can fill it in fast after a real round.

**Acceptance.** `behavioral/follow-up-email.md` with the template and one fully-filled-in example.

---

## Task 6 — Reflection

Write 5–8 sentences addressing:

1. Which category is your **weakest** going into Mock #3 — the one where your story is thinnest or your delivery shakiest? Name it.
2. What is the one number across your whole story bank you're least sure of, and how will you verify or honestly re-frame it?
3. Which felt harder this week — *finding* the stories, *structuring* them in STAR, or *delivering* them aloud? Why?
4. One concrete thing you'll drill before the behavioral round in Mock #3.

The reflection is the portfolio-grade artifact — it's what turns this week from "I wrote some stories" into "I know exactly what to sharpen next." Future you will thank present you.

**Acceptance.** `behavioral/reflection.md`.

---

## Acceptance

The week's homework is complete when:

- Twelve-plus stories are committed under `behavioral/story-bank/`, each in STAR with a quantified result.
- The full-round recording (Challenge 1) is linked, scored, and reviewed in two passes.
- `coverage-matrix.md` shows no empty column across the eight categories.
- The 90-second pitch is written and recorded, under 90 seconds.
- The follow-up-email template and the reflection are committed.
- The quiz is taken and scored; the weakest category is named in the reflection.
- A push log shows daily commits Mon–Sun.

The single most useful artifact this week is the **coverage matrix with no empty column** — it is the difference between walking into Mock #3 (and the real onsites after) knowing you have a strong story for every question, versus inventing one under pressure. The category most learners find thinnest after W13 is "difficult person" (the empathy-not-blame framing is hard) or "failure" (the instinct to self-protect). Whichever is yours, drill it aloud until the story comes out clean.
