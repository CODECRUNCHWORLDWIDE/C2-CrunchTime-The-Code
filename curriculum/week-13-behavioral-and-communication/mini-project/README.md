## The Brief

The week's deliverable is a **story bank**: twelve or more refined stories, each
cross-referenced to the categories it can answer, anchored by a coverage matrix
that proves every one of the eight categories is covered, with a recording of
each story delivered aloud.

Most of the stories already exist. Five came from this week's exercises, four
from your Week 1 and Week 4 homework, and three are new. The work here is
refining them to one consistent bar, cross-referencing them, building the matrix
and recording them. By Sunday you have one folder you can re-read the morning of
any onsite.

The reason this matters more than twelve separate scripts:

Three reasons.

1. **Phase 4 grades the full loop, not just the coding.** Mock #3 next week is the first simulation that includes a behavioral round; Mock #4 in W15 is the dress rehearsal. The story bank you build now is *used* under timed pressure starting next week. Engineers who freeze in behavioral rounds are almost always engineers who tried to invent the answer in real time — the bank converts the cognitive load from *invention* (slow, freezes people) to *selection* (fast, reliable).

2. **A bank covers what eight scripts cannot.** In a real loop you will have already used your best story in an earlier round, and you cannot tell the same story to two interviewers who compare notes. Twelve stories covering eight categories with redundancy mean you can always reach for a fresh one. The reuse principle — one story answers several categories depending on which beat you emphasize — is what makes twelve stories cover eight categories several times over.

3. **The coverage matrix is the proof of completeness.** It turns "do I have enough stories?" from a vague worry into a checkable invariant: every column has at least one ●, or you have a gap to fill. Walking into an onsite with a complete matrix is the difference between confidence and hope.

### The coverage matrix

`coverage-matrix.md` is a grid: your twelve stories down the rows, the eight categories across the columns, a ● where a story strongly covers a category and a ○ where it's usable but not your best. The format and an illustrative example are in [Lecture 2 §6](../lecture-notes/02-the-star-method-and-the-story-bank.md).

Two ways to read it, both required:

- **Down each column** — every category must have at least one ●. An empty column is a gap; fill it before you call the bank done. This is the invariant the matrix exists to check.
- **Across each row** — see how flexible each story is. A story covering five or six categories is a workhorse worth polishing first; a story covering one (often the dedicated failure story) is a fine specialist.

Below the grid, add a short "**deployment notes**" section: for each of the eight categories, name your *first-choice* story and your *backup* (for the case where you already used the first choice in an earlier round). This is what you re-read the morning of an onsite.

---

### How to use the bank

Write a short usage section so future-you can pick it up cold:

- **The morning of an onsite:** re-read the coverage matrix and the deployment notes, not all twelve stories. You want the *index* fresh, not a last-minute cram.
- **In the room:** hear the prompt → name the category (5 sec) → select the story (not invent) → deliver in 90 seconds STAR → land the number.
- **Across the loop:** track which story you used in which round so you don't repeat. The redundancy in the matrix is what lets you not repeat.

### Rubric

The story bank is graded on six dimensions. Total possible: 100 points; passing: 70.

| Dimension | Points | What "full credit" looks like |
|-----------|-------:|-------------------------------|
| Completeness | 20 | Twelve-plus stories, each in the STAR template; recordings linked for each |
| Coverage | 20 | Matrix present; **no empty column** across all eight categories; deployment notes (first-choice + backup) per category |
| STAR structure | 15 | Every story holds the 10/10/60/20 shape; Action is the bulk; no S/T over-spend |
| Quantified results | 15 | Every story's Result leads with a number (or an honest labeled estimate) tied to something that mattered |
| "I vs we" discipline | 10 | "I" for contribution, "we" for outcome, consistently; ownership and collaboration both surfaced |
| Delivery (recordings) | 20 | Each story recorded from memory, ~90 seconds, tightened on a second take; rehearsal notes present |

A passing bank scores at least 70 overall **and** has no empty column in the matrix — the coverage invariant is a hard gate, not a soft point total. A beautiful bank that can't answer a "difficult person" question is not done.

## Constraints

- **No empty column in the matrix.** This is a hard gate, not a point total. A
  beautiful bank that cannot answer a "difficult person" question is not done.
- **No story told twice in one loop.** That is what the redundancy is for, and it
  is why twelve stories rather than eight.
- **Every Result leads with a number**, or with an honest labelled estimate. "It
  went well" is not a result.
- **"I" for the contribution, "we" for the outcome**, consistently. Both signals
  are graded and they pull in opposite directions, which is why the discipline
  has to be deliberate.
- **Recorded from memory.** A story you can only read is not in the bank.
- **The stories are true.** Every one of them will meet a follow-up question
  eventually, and follow-ups find seams.

## Expected output

What a finished bank looks like, as a set of numbers you can check:

```text
stories                 12+, each in the STAR template
categories covered      8 of 8, every column with at least one strong story
deployment notes        8 rows: first choice and backup, per category
recordings              one per story, ~90s, from memory
workhorse stories       2-3 covering five or six categories each
specialists             1-2 covering one category well (the failure story usually)
rubric                  70+ of 100, and no empty column
```

The shape to expect is a few workhorses and a few specialists, not twelve equal
stories. A story that answers five categories is worth polishing first; one that
answers only the failure question is a perfectly good specialist and should not
be forced to stretch.

## Steps

1. Gather the nine existing stories into the folder, unedited, first. Seeing them
   together is what tells you what is missing.
2. Build the matrix from those nine, honestly. Mark a strong cover and a usable
   cover differently.
3. Read down the columns. The empty ones are your Task 1: they tell you which
   three stories to write, rather than you guessing.
4. Write stories 10 and 11 to fill those gaps, and story 12 as the through-line —
   the one that answers "why you".
5. Refine all twelve to the same bar: quantified result, tagged categories,
   follow-ups listed.
6. Rebuild the matrix. Check every column again.
7. Write the deployment notes: first choice and backup, per category.
8. Record all twelve from memory. Re-take the ones that ran long.
9. Write the bank's README: index, how to use it, and the reflection.

## The Solution

The worked answer for this page is not a story — you have five of those already,
under `## The Solution` on each exercise page. It is the **matrix**, because that
is the part everyone builds badly on the first attempt.

Here is a worked one, filled in for a bank of twelve. `●` is a strong cover, `○`
is usable but not your best:

```text
                          acc  con  fail  lead  amb  diff  team  why
story-01 checkout latency   ●    ○    ○     ○    ●    .     ○     .
story-02 datastore choice   .    ●    .     ○    ○    ●     ●     .
story-03 flaky CI           ●    ○    .     ●    ○    .     ●     .
story-04 migration cutover  .    .    ●     .    .    .     ○     .
story-05 the vague dashboard ○   .    .     ○    ●    .     ○     .
story-06 on-call handover   ○    ○    ○     ●    .     ●    ●     .
story-07 the rewrite we cut ○    ●    ○     ○    ●    .     ○     .
story-08 mentoring a junior .    ○    .     ●    .     ●    ●     ○
story-09 the vendor switch  ●    ●    .     ○    ●    ○     .     .
story-10 the missed launch  .    ○    ●     .    ○    ○     ○     .
story-11 the difficult peer .    ●    .     ○    .     ●    ●     .
story-12 why this work      .    .    ○     .    .     .     .    ●
                           ---  ---  ---   ---  ---  ---   ---   ---
strong covers               3    4    2     3    3     4     5    1
```

Read it twice, and both readings matter.

**Down the columns.** Every column has at least one `●`, so there is no gap. Two
columns are thin: `fail` has two and `why` has one. That is a finding, not a
failure — it tells you that if a loop asks the failure question twice, you are
reaching for your second-best, and it tells you story-12 has no backup at all.
Either write a thirteenth story or accept the risk knowingly.

**Across the rows.** Story-06 and story-07 answer five categories each; those are
the workhorses and they get polished first. Story-04 answers exactly one. That is
fine — the failure story is a specialist by nature, and stretching it to cover
"teamwork" would weaken the thing it is actually good at.

The deployment notes then fall out of the grid in eight lines:

```text
accomplishment   first: story-01   backup: story-09
conflict         first: story-02   backup: story-11
failure          first: story-04   backup: story-10
leadership       first: story-03   backup: story-08
ambiguity        first: story-05   backup: story-09
difficult person first: story-11   backup: story-06
teamwork         first: story-06   backup: story-08
why this work    first: story-12   backup: -- gap, accepted
```

That block, not the twelve stories, is what you re-read on the morning of an
onsite. It is eight lines and it converts the room's cognitive load from
invention — which is slow and makes people freeze — into selection, which is
fast and reliable.

The bank lives in your portfolio repo, in the layout under Requirements above.

Record each story from memory, roughly ninety seconds, and re-take the ones that

### Self-reflection

End the bank's `README.md` with a short reflection — 4–6 sentences — addressing:

1. Which story is your strongest workhorse (covers the most categories), and why?
2. Which category was hardest to find a real story for, and what did you do about it?
3. What was harder this week — finding the stories, structuring them in STAR, or delivering them aloud?
4. The one thing you want to drill before the behavioral round in **Mock #3** next week.

The reflection is the portfolio-grade artifact. It's also the bridge to next week: the category you name as weakest here is the one to rehearse before Mock #3.

## Common bugs to catch

- **Building the matrix last.** Symptom: twelve stories and three of them
  redundant, because nothing told you what was missing until the end. Build it
  from nine and let it tell you what to write.
- **Marking every cell as a strong cover.** Symptom: a matrix with no gaps and a
  bank that fails in the room. Be strict about `●`.
- **Twelve stories, all workhorses.** Symptom: every story stretched to answer
  everything, and none of them sharp. Specialists are fine.
- **Re-reading all twelve on the morning.** Symptom: a last-minute cram and a
  fogged index. The deployment notes are the thing to re-read.
- **Recording from the page.** Symptom: a bank you can read and not tell.
- **Leaving the Week 1 and Week 4 stories at their original bar.** Symptom: four
  visibly weaker stories that you will not reach for, which means the bank is
  really eight.
- **A "why this work" story with no backup and no note.** Symptom: an accepted
  risk you never actually accepted, because you never noticed it.

## Stretch

- Add a thirteenth story for whichever column is thinnest. The matrix already
  told you which one.
- Write, for each of the eight categories, the **follow-up you would least like**
  and a two-sentence answer to it. That set is what turns a bank into something
  that survives a real interviewer.
- Deliver the whole bank in one sitting — twelve stories back to back, recorded.
  It is roughly twenty minutes and it is the most honest test of whether they are
  in memory.
- Re-read the bank in three months and cut the two weakest. A bank that only ever
  grows becomes an archive.
