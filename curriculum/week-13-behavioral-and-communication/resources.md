# Week 13 — Resources

This is a **non-coding week.** The PEP 8 and Big-O references that recur in the algorithm weeks do not apply here — drop them. The references below are about behavioral interviewing, communication, and negotiation. Every free resource is publicly accessible; the books are widely available through libraries and bookstores.

## Required reading (work it into your week)

- **Tech Interview Handbook — Behavioral Interview section** (free): <https://www.techinterviewhandbook.org/behavioral-interview/> — the single best free, structured treatment of the behavioral round. It enumerates the common question categories, walks the STAR method, and gives a question bank with sample answers. Read the overview, the "STAR" page, and at least three of the per-category pages (conflict, failure, teamwork) during Monday and Tuesday. This is the canonical free reference for everything in Lecture 1 and Lecture 2.
- **Tech Interview Handbook — main site** (free): <https://www.techinterviewhandbook.org/> — the parent site. Its "Interview cheatsheet" and "Resume" sections are useful context for the whole onsite loop; skim them once.
- **Amazon Leadership Principles** (free): <https://www.amazon.jobs/content/en/our-workplace/leadership-principles> — the sixteen principles Amazon's "bar raiser" interviewers grade behavioral answers against. Even if you are not interviewing at Amazon, this is the most explicit public statement of what a behavioral rubric measures. Map each of your story-bank anecdotes to one or two principles as the Stretch goal in the README suggests; "Ownership," "Dive Deep," "Earn Trust," and "Deliver Results" are the near-universal axes.
- **interviewing.io blog** (free): <https://interviewing.io/blog> — real data and write-ups from anonymous mock interviews, including behavioral-round breakdowns and what differentiates a strong answer from a weak one. Read one or two posts on behavioral signal; the data-driven framing complements the rubric in Lecture 1.

## On structuring answers — STAR and the story bank

- **Tech Interview Handbook — "Self introduction"** (free): <https://www.techinterviewhandbook.org/behavioral-interview-questions/> — the per-question bank, including "tell me about yourself." Use it to stress-test your story bank: for each listed question, can you name which of your twelve stories you would deploy? If a listed question has no story behind it, you have found a gap in your coverage matrix.
- **"Cracking the Coding Interview" by Gayle Laakmann McDowell** — the behavioral chapter ("Behavioral Questions") introduces the preparation grid: list your projects down one axis and the common question types across the other, then fill in which story fits each cell. That grid is exactly this week's coverage matrix; the book is the original source for the technique and is worth owning for the whole interview prep arc, not just this week.
- **"Staff Engineer" by Will Larson** — <https://staffeng.com/> — the "Stories" section of the companion site collects real narratives from staff-plus engineers about scope, influence, and impact. Reading a few is the fastest way to calibrate what a high-ownership, high-impact story sounds like at the senior level — the bar your story bank is aiming at.

## On communication under pressure and difficult conversations

- **"Crucial Conversations" by Kerry Patterson, Joseph Grenny, Ron McMillan, and Al Switzler** — the canonical book on high-stakes, high-emotion conversations. The "Start with Heart," "Learn to Look," and "Make It Safe" frameworks are directly applicable to the conflict, difficult-person, and hostile-question material in Lecture 3. The behavioral round is, after all, a high-stakes conversation; the techniques transfer.
- **"Never Split the Difference" by Chris Voss** — the FBI-negotiator book on tactical empathy, calibrated questions, and lowering the emotional temperature. The "How am I supposed to do that?" calibrated question and the mirroring/labeling techniques are exactly the moves for handling a hardball follow-up or a compensation question without going adversarial. Read the chapters on calibrated questions and labeling.
- **"The Manager's Path" by Camille Fournier** — written for engineers stepping into management, but the chapters on managing up, navigating disagreement with your manager, and giving and receiving feedback are the best available source on the *substance* behind conflict and difficult-person stories. If your conflict story involves a manager or a cross-team disagreement, this book will sharpen how you frame it.

## On reflection and career narrative

- **"Designing Your Life" by Bill Burnett & Dave Evans** — the Stanford d.school book on applying design thinking to your career. The "Good Time Journal" and "Workview / Lifeview" exercises are a structured way to surface the experiences that become your proudest-accomplishment and why-this-role stories — and to articulate the through-line that turns "tell me about yourself" from a résumé recitation into a narrative. Use it for the reflection homework and the 90-second pitch.

## On the eight categories

The surface forms of behavioral prompts collapse into eight underlying categories. The recognition skill is mapping a prompt's wording to the category it tests, then to the story that covers it.

| Category | Canonical prompts | Top signal graded |
|----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Conflict | "Tell me about a disagreement with a coworker / your manager." | Collaboration + self-awareness; did you resolve it without damage? |
| Failure / mistake | "Tell me about a time you failed." | Ownership + growth; do you own it and name the lesson? |
| Leadership / influence | "Tell me about a time you led without authority." | Ownership + collaboration; did you move people without a title? |
| Ambiguity | "Tell me about a time the requirements were unclear." | Ownership + impact; did you create clarity and ship? |
| Teamwork / collaboration | "Tell me about a time you worked on a team." | Collaboration; what did *you* contribute to a shared win? |
| Difficult person | "Tell me about a difficult person you worked with." | Self-awareness + collaboration; empathy, not blame. |
| Biggest accomplishment | "What is the work you're proudest of?" | Impact + ownership; a quantified, owned result. |
| Why this role / tell me about yourself | "Walk me through your background." / "Why us?" | Communication + fit; a clear narrative and genuine motivation. |

Most prompts map to one category. Some are deliberate hybrids — "Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager about a technical decision and what happened" is Conflict + Leadership + (often) Result. Naming the hybrid and choosing a story that lands all of its beats is itself a senior signal.

## On the STAR budget

The four-part structure and the time you should spend on each part. The whole answer targets **ninety seconds.**

| Part | What it is | Target length | The trap |
|------|------------|--------------:|----------|
| Situation | The context — team, project, what was at stake | ~10 sec | Over-explaining the setup; the interviewer does not need the org chart |
| Task | Your specific responsibility or the problem you owned | ~10 sec | Confusing the team's task with *your* task |
| Action | What **you** did, step by step — the technical and interpersonal moves | ~60 sec | Under-spending here; this is where every signal lives |
| Result | The quantified outcome plus what you learned | ~20 sec | No number; trailing off; forgetting the lesson |

The single most common behavioral mistake is the inverse budget: forty-five seconds of Situation, ten seconds of Action, no Result. Fix the budget and you fix most of the round.

## On the five signals

The rubric behind every behavioral round. A strong answer surfaces three or four of these; a weak answer surfaces none.

| Signal | What the interviewer is checking | How to surface it |
|--------|----------------------------------|-------------------|
| Ownership | Did you drive it, or watch it happen? | "I" language; you made the call, you took the risk, you stayed late |
| Impact | Did it matter, and can you prove it? | A number: latency, dollars, incidents, percent, time, people unblocked |
| Self-awareness | Do you know your own edges? | A genuine "what I'd do differently"; a real weakness with a system around it |
| Collaboration | Can you work with and through people? | "We" for the outcome; crediting others; resolving conflict without damage |
| Growth | Did the experience change how you operate? | "Since then, I always…"; a habit or process you adopted because of it |

## Glossary additions

- **Behavioral round** — an interview round, usually 30–60 minutes, in which the interviewer asks "tell me about a time…" questions and grades the answers against a signal rubric. As scorable as a coding round, despite feeling "soft."
- **STAR** — Situation, Task, Action, Result. The four-part structure for a behavioral answer. The behavioral analogue of the UMPIRE method: a mechanical scaffold that frees attention for signal.
- **Story bank** — a curated set of twelve-plus refined, rehearsed STAR anecdotes drawn from your real experience, each cross-referenced to the categories it covers. The asset this week builds.
- **Coverage matrix** — a grid of stories (rows) against the eight categories (columns), with a mark in each cell a story covers. Used to verify every category has at least one story behind it.
- **Signal** — a gradable attribute of a behavioral answer: ownership, impact, self-awareness, collaboration, growth. Interviewers score signals, not stories.
- **The "I vs we" discipline** — using "I" for your individual contribution and "we" for the team outcome. Taking credit for your part without grabbing credit for others'.
- **The recovery move** — the technique for fixing a behavioral answer mid-stream when you realize you picked the wrong story or buried the result: a brief, honest reset ("Actually, let me give you a sharper example…") rather than pushing through a weak answer.
- **Non-adversarial reframe** — treating the interviewer as someone looking for a reason to hire you, so that a hostile-sounding follow-up reads as a depth probe rather than an attack.
- **Calibrated question** — an open-ended "how" or "what" question (from *Never Split the Difference*) that keeps the other person engaged and lowers the temperature; useful for hardball follow-ups and compensation questions.
- **The follow-up email** — a short, same-day note after the interview referencing one specific thing from the conversation. Low effort, high leverage, frequently skipped.
- **Bar raiser** — Amazon's term for an interviewer trained to grade against the Leadership Principles and protect the hiring bar; by extension, any rubric-driven behavioral interviewer.

## Cheatsheet — the 30-second behavioral pre-flight

Before any behavioral round, walk this in your head:

```
What category is this prompt?    -> Conflict / Failure / Leadership / Ambiguity /
                                    Teamwork / Difficult-person / Accomplishment /
                                    Why-this-role. Name it in 5 seconds.

Which story do I deploy?         -> Pull from the bank. Pick the one whose ACTION
                                    beats land the category's top signal.

What is the budget?              -> 10s Situation, 10s Task, 60s Action, 20s Result.
                                    Ninety seconds total.

What is the number?              -> Every story has a quantified result. Lead the
                                    Result with it. No number = no score on impact.

Is the "I" clear?                -> "I" for my contribution, "we" for the outcome.

What is the lesson?              -> End on growth: "since then, I always…"

If I freeze or pick wrong?       -> Restate the question, breathe, or reset:
                                    "Actually, a sharper example is…"
```

Read aloud; should hit 25–30 seconds. The order matters — it is the same order the answer comes out in.
